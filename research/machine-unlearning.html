When “forget this data” breaks the model

Machine unlearning removes the influence of some training examples without retraining from scratch. I studied a popular shortcut, NegGrad+, and found that it becomes unreliable when the examples to forget all come from the same class.

The setting

A small convolutional network is trained on Fashion-MNIST (55,000 photos of clothes, 10 classes). Then 600 of these photos must be forgotten. The gold standard is a model retrained from scratch without them; approximate methods try to get close to it much faster.

The question is simple: with the same number of photos to forget, does it matter whether they all come from one class (concentrated) or are spread evenly over the ten classes (spread)? In the concentrated case, the other photos of that class stay in the training data: the model should forget these 600 boots, not boots in general.

Build a deletion request

During unlearning, the photos to forget are fed to the network in batches of 64. Each BatchNorm layer rescales its signals using the mean and variance of the current batch. A concentrated request therefore produces batches made of a single class, whose statistics are very different from those of a normal, mixed batch. That turned out to matter.

Why NegGrad+ can run away

NegGrad+ minimises α·CE(keep) − (1−α)·CE(forget): it keeps practising on the data that stays, and pushes the error on the data to forget up. The error used is the cross-entropy, CE = −log p, where p is the probability the model gives to the true class.

Push the probability of the true class down

As p goes to 0, −log p grows without limit: nothing stops the push. In the experiments, a forget loss above 10 counts as a divergence. A control method that instead teaches random wrong labels has a finite target and cannot run away in the same way.

What I found

1 · Concentration makes NegGrad+ unstable

In a prospective study with 10 independent repetitions (seeds) and 68 mixes of 1, 2, 5 or 10 classes, NegGrad+ diverged mostly when the request was concentrated. Hover the bars.

Runs out of: 100 one-class, 450 two-class, 120 five-class and 10 ten-class sets (implementation A, weight 0.01). A variant that keeps BatchNorm in evaluation mode (C) never diverged or collapsed in this study.

2 · It is not just that some classes are harder

A statistical model accounted for the difficulty of each class and for a whole family of ways to combine them (power means of order −3 to 3). Even then, less concentrated sets had a lower early forget loss than their classes predict: −0.246 decade per bit of label entropy (95% CI −0.263 to −0.228, all 10 seeds negative). A decade is a factor of 10. This rules out the combination rules of that family, not every possible interaction between specific pairs of classes.

3 · It is specific to how NegGrad+ forgets

With everything else identical, I compared NegGrad+ with the random-label control. The measure is the typical size of the gradients before they are clipped (Ḡ, in decades): how much more it grows for concentrated than for spread requests.

Can it be avoided?

Three pre-specified follow-up studies tried to remove the penalty: how much more accuracy on the remaining data the model loses with concentrated requests than with spread ones (in percentage points, after recomputing BatchNorm statistics). Each dot is one seed.

Inside the tuning study

For each class, 20 settings (4 learning rates × 5 forget weights) were tried on 3 seeds. A setting counted as stable if no run diverged or collapsed, and as feasible if it also reached the forgetting strength of the retrained model. Pick a class.

Forgetting strength (decades) of the chosen setting vs the retrained model.

Reading. Tuning brought the penalty from about 14 points to about 1, but for 7 of 10 classes no stable setting reached the retrained model's strength: the tuned method mostly acted less. The BatchNorm fix behaved similarly: it protected the model but barely changed its predictions on the data to forget.

A measurement trap

Many runs looked “collapsed” (test accuracy ≤ 20%). But that accuracy is measured with the statistics BatchNorm stored during unlearning, which the single-class batches had corrupted. Recompute those statistics on the remaining data, without touching any weight, and see what happens.

A real loss remains (about 74% vs 93% for the original model), but much smaller than it first appeared. How an unlearned model is evaluated can change the conclusion.

How it was done

It began with an unexpected failure, continued with exploratory diagnostics, and then moved to studies whose plan was fixed before any result was seen:

  • Protocol: the question, one main test and the wording of each possible conclusion.
  • Simulations: how many seeds are needed (power), and whether the test raises false alarms (calibration).
  • Freeze: the plan and the code are committed with a fingerprint before the run.
  • Pilot: only to measure computing time; its results stay sealed.
  • Study and frozen analysis: run on a cloud GPU (A100), analysed exactly as written.

Each seed trains a new model, and the statistics treat one seed as one data point. The studies share some data, so they are not fully independent replications, and the plans live in a private repository rather than a public registry.

Lessons and limits

If you deploy unlearning

  • Look at the class composition of deletion requests.
  • Test the method on concentrated requests: spread ones never showed the failure.
  • Report divergence, collapse, accuracy loss and forgetting strength separately.
  • Do not assume that tuning will save you.

What this does not show

  • No study measured true erasure or privacy.
  • BatchNorm is a suspect, not a proven mechanism.
  • Mostly one small CNN on Fashion-MNIST, with smaller checks on CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet.
